01 · Field research · Team alignment

Nodes, suckout, and hubs. Oh my.

Deep understanding of user mindset gives team a shared language for communication

Client
Cox Communications
Users
Field technicians repairing physical cable infrastructure
Methods
Ride-alongs, field observation, in-depth SME interviews
Outcome
One decision question every team could use

Cox Communications wanted to update the legacy tools field technicians used to identify, locate, and repair problems in the physical cable infrastructure. The existing tools had accumulated over time and required technicians to work across multiple applications, windows, and data requests. They ran on outdated systems with limited bandwidth and display capabilities.

Extensive field research with the technicians was conducted and SME’s were interviewed at length. There were multiple stakeholder groups involved and each group could see its concern of the moment as the most important. Discussions about features and priorities could sink into technical minutiae while the shared project goal disappeared from view.

The teams needed a shared way to talk about the user goals and we found this in understanding how the technicians think about their work. They follow a differential diagnosis method. Doctors use this method to diagnose a patient by eliminating explanations that do not account for the issue. Field technicians work in a similar way as they troubleshoot: they consider possibilities and eliminate what does not explain the problem. Understanding this thought process gave the team a shorthand for the many things a technician is thinking through while trying to make a repair.

It was high-level enough to be easy for different groups to use, but had enough technical substance for people working in a highly specialized domain to trust it. I started discussing the idea with my team, tried it with SMEs, and then presented it to the combined teams. Once identified, this framework became the team's shared language. Instead of debating features or process, teams could ask: Does this decision help the technicians reach a more certain diagnosis more quickly?

The developers were especially grateful. The developers knew the details but not the big picture. It was like knowing grammar and how sentences should be constructed without knowing what the words meant. I explained that context, helping developers see how their tools fit into the technicians' troubleshooting process. The shared framework made it easier to discuss competing priorities and, as I observed, easier to make product decisions.

02 · Design discovery · Coaching

Of Mice and...Zippers

Supportive mentoring empowers new designer

Client
Cox Communications
Project
Firmware updates for millions of cable modems
My role
Research and design discovery lead; mentor to a junior designer
Methods
User interviews, observation, iterative working sessions

Cox Communications needed to redesign how millions of cable modems received firmware updates. The current process was manual, error-prone, and had once pushed an error onto thousands of modems, costing several million dollars to correct. The redesign required bridging a gap between very busy and technical users, inexperienced designers, and developers.

I led the research and design discovery project, but my real focus was coaching a junior designer transitioning from fashion design into software. She had strong design instincts but had never worked with users and was new to interactive design. I started out leading the user interviews and working sessions modeling how to establish rapport, dig deep on the reasoning behind their work practices, and let them be the experts. We worked through many interviews, observations, and iterative design sessions together. I let her lead increasingly, always there to help her navigate uncertainty and feel supported.

Her excellent redesign reduced the process from 13 steps using 5 tools to 6 steps using 1 tool with appropriate automation. She really made huge strides forward in her ability to work with users and incorporate their needs into her designs. She was moving from a static world of clothing design into the dynamic and interactive world of software and that was a challenge for her. She had to learn how to always be thinking of what came before and after every design decision and how the users might react in an unexpected way. The proudest moment for me was walking into our war-room and finding her holding court with the three very technical and older users as they worked through a sticky design issue together. She later posted on LinkedIn about how empowered she felt through this project.

This project made clear to me that my best work is often done through others and that the opportunity to pass along my hard-won skills and experiences is very important to me.

03 · Recruiting · Research rigor

Who's brokering the deal?

Figuring out who really does the work

Client
Major insurance and financial services company
Users
Brokerage staff who service client policies
Methods
About 20 staff interviews, recruited by broker referral
Outcome
Personas and findings used across a multi-year project

The product team at a major insurance and financial services company wanted to understand how brokers used its online tools to manage customer accounts, process claims, and service policies. The research would uncover workflow issues, evaluate proposed features, and help guide product priorities. During planning, licensed brokers who sold policies were identified as the primary research participants.

Recruiting those brokers proved difficult, with a very low acceptance rate. Early interviews revealed that only at the smallest brokerages did brokers themselves use the tools to service client policies. At most brokerages, staff handled that work. We needed to change our recruiting target. Stakeholders were under pressure to stay on schedule and initially wanted to proceed with whichever interviews we could get.

Despite the pressure, I advocated for research rigor while recognizing the time already invested in scoping and planning. I worked with stakeholders to revise the schedule so we could share preliminary findings with the teams as interviews took place. Asking brokers to recommend their staff members who used our online tools became an effective way to recruit participants.

With the right participants identified and recruited, the team completed about 20 staff interviews. The resulting personas, findings, and recommendations were useful to product teams throughout what became a multi-year project.

By balancing rigorous research with the team’s need for early design input, I helped make the findings useful throughout the project—not just at the conclusion of the research phase. On an organizational level, the maturity of the teams involved was increased and set the stage for smoother collaboration in the future.

04 · Qualitative analysis · AI evaluation

Talented rookie falls short

Expert humans outpace AI in real world

Client
Sun Life
Study
Customer research on a specialized, complex insurance product
Data
20 one-hour interviews
AI tools
Early 2026 versions of Claude, general and domain-trained

Sun Life needed to conduct research with its customers about a very specialized and complex type of insurance product. A team of experienced researchers including two specialists in this product was assembled and together they developed a research plan and interview guides. During this study it was decided to assess the utility of a general AI tool for analyzing interview transcripts in this somewhat niche domain. Most of the AI work was done using one or more early 2026 versions of Claude.

Two team members conducted one hour interviews with 20 participants resulting in a large body of transcript data.

The team members had various levels of familiarity with using AI for this kind of work and used prompts of varying sophistication in their analysis. However, without specialized domain knowledge the AI assistant made it SLOWER for the experienced researchers to make progress through the mass of qualitative data being analyzed. The assistant did not understand terms of art, often overlooked significant issues, and often created statements that grammatically made sense, but weren’t factually correct. So much time was spent correcting or modifying the output that the team decided it was faster to do it manually.

Because the lead researcher already had in mind a framework for the findings and had decided upon a strategic solution for the company to follow, she was able to analyze and interpret the data and put it into this framework much more quickly than the other researchers. Also, because the AI assistant did NOT have this framework as context, it was of limited use in connecting the findings in the data to the framework and strategy.

I created a custom AI assistant trained on hundreds of pages of documentation about this product. This made it much more useful for extracting themes and patterns from the qualitative data. But without the lead researcher’s framework and strategic outlook, the output from the trained AI assistant was still lacking in its impact on the communication of the findings to the stakeholders. Without the strategic framework of the human analysts it stuck closely to findings which were supported by the data. This was not without utility, but the time and effort required to train the custom agent made it impractical for accelerating a single project.

05 · AI tooling · Researcher development

Who'll coach the coach?

Status
In use at a leading HCI graduate program; development ongoing
Users
Graduate students learning user-centered research and evaluation
Training data
Real interviews, real transcripts, my evaluations of both
Focus
Interviewing skills that transfer across domains

Many organizations are experimenting with AI to increase the speed with which they can analyze large amounts of qualitative data. While a rigorously trained agent or assistant can be useful, as demonstrated in another case study in this portfolio, the time and effort needed to train the assistant may not be available for a given study. It may be that skilled researchers are still faster and more efficient for one-off research studies. For repeated research studies or data analysis within a domain, it can be worth the effort to train an assistant.

However, the generation of data for analysis still depends on the skill of the researcher. Training an agent to teach the skills involved in conducting qualitative research, especially one-on-one interviews can be broadly useful across any domain. Humans with specific domain knowledge can be given coaching at their own pace and on their schedule to help them become good enough interviewers within that domain. You see this approach in spaceflight when domain specialists are trained to be astronauts rather than the other way around. An expert geologist (for example) can become a good-enough astronaut to safely crew a mission to the moon and bring with them all their expertise in geology.

In addition, a rigorously trained AI assistant can be helpful in coaching junior researchers to improve their interviewing skills. By taking the time to engage in extended training sessions with an agent to give it the knowledge gained from years of experience, the AI assistant can be made into a useful coach. Using real interviews and real transcripts and teaching the agent how the human would evaluate the same, the agent can gain a level of competence sufficient to help less experienced researchers benefit from the trainer’s years of work. This skill can be transferred to experts in any domain so that their interviews with people about that domain are free of bias and provide a supportive environment so that subjects feel comfortable sharing their true experiences.

This researcher has developed an expert interview coach agent which is currently being utilized by a leading Human Computer Interaction graduate program to coach students who are learning how to conduct user centered research and evaluation methods. The agent takes their interview transcripts and identifies their strengths and weaknesses across several dimensions. It can advise on better ways to ask questions, better ways to follow up on missed opportunities, and provides coaching on better interview design. The agent can also be utilized when planning studies to develop stronger questions and interview guides appropriate to the scope and format of the study.

Continued development of this agent is ongoing through utilizing results and feedback from the initial test with graduate students.
